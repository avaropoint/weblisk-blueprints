## What this job is for

This position performs very little recurring work, and that is not a gap — it is
the shape of the job. What arrives here is other people's overdue work and other
people's finished work: the escalations when a clock runs out, and the
signatures that put documents in force or accept records. It is the backstop and
the authority, which is why so many paths end at it.

Which means the failure mode is not *doing too little*. It is being the place
where things arrive and stop.

## Your first week

1. **Find out how much escalates here, and from whom.** The volume is the
   finding. If a great deal escalates to this position, the layer beneath it is
   understaffed, not careless — and adding a reminder will not fix it.
2. **Find out what is waiting on a signature.** A document that has been drafted,
   reviewed and never adopted is not in force. Nobody is working to it, and the
   organisation believes it is covered.
3. **Ask what has *not* been escalated.** Every position that escalates here has
   made a judgement about what is worth your attention, and that judgement is
   where the real risk is being held.
4. **Read one thing end to end** — one procedure and the records it produced over
   a year. It will tell you more about whether the organisation works than any
   dashboard.

## What good looks like

- **Escalations are rare and each one is real.** A steady trickle means the
  clocks are set right. A flood means the layer beneath cannot cope. Silence
  means either everything is on time or nothing is being measured, and those
  look identical from here.
- **Signatures are timely, and they are not automatic.** A signature given
  without reading is worse than no signature, because it is the organisation
  formally asserting something nobody checked. An approval queue with a
  same-day average and no rejections is a rubber stamp with a date on it.
- **Somebody tells you bad news early, unprompted, more than once a year.** If
  nobody does, either nothing is going wrong or the organisation has learned
  what happens to people who say so.
- **The programme survives a departure.** The measure of this position's work is
  what still runs when a key person leaves.

## How it goes wrong

- **Becoming the bottleneck that nobody names.** Work escalates here, waits, and
  the people beneath adapt by not escalating. The queue goes down and the risk
  goes up, and every visible number improves.
- **Signing without reading.** The most consequential failure available to this
  position and the least effortful.
- **Commitment stated and not resourced.** A policy signed at this level and a
  budget that does not fund it is a documented decision to accept the risk, and
  it will be read that way afterwards by people who were not in the room.
- **Reading only the summary.** Every layer between the work and this position
  rounds towards reassurance. Not dishonestly — each person softens by a little,
  and four layers of a little is a different picture.
- **Treating an escalation as a report.** It is not information; it is a
  transfer. When an obligation escalates here it has become this position's to
  discharge, and a note acknowledging it discharges nothing.

## What you cannot hand to anybody else

Accepting a document into force. This is not an administrative step, and it is
deliberately not derivable from who did the work: the person who performs an
activity is rarely the person who should judge that it is the right activity.
Delegating adoption collapses that separation, and the organisation loses the
only checkpoint it has between *somebody wrote this* and *this is what we do*.

And the decision to accept a risk rather than fix it. That decision is
legitimate, it is frequently correct, and it belongs here in writing. Made
quietly further down, by someone who lacked the authority to fund the
alternative, it is not a decision at all — it is an omission that will be read
as one afterwards.
