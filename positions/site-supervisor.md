## What this job is for

You are the point where a written programme becomes a thing that happens to a
person. Whatever the table above says, the character of this work is that it
cannot be done from a desk and cannot be caught up later. A supervisor's work is
the only work in a programme that is genuinely perishable: a check not made this
morning cannot be made this afternoon, because the thing it was checking has
already been used.

## Your first week

1. **Learn what has to happen before work starts, and refuse to start without
   it.** The first time you let it slide you have taught the crew what the rule
   actually is, and you will not get that back this season.
2. **Do one of every recurring check yourself, on your worst day, in bad
   weather.** Anything that cannot be done properly in ten minutes with cold
   hands will not be done properly, and you now know which ones to fix rather
   than which ones to nag about.
3. **Find out which of your checks multiply.** Some are one per organisation and
   some are one *per project*. Where the organisation is running several at once
   the same rule is several pieces of work, and the plan that assumed one is the
   plan that quietly fails.
4. **Meet whoever your lateness escalates to, before anything is late.** The
   first conversation you have with them should not be about a missed deadline.
5. **Ask your crew what they would not report to you.** Whatever they name is
   what is actually happening.

## What good looks like

- **Checks are made before the work, not written up after it.** The paperwork is
  a by-product; when it becomes the product, the work stops being checked.
- **Somebody covers your checks when you are off, and it is arranged in
  advance.** A daily obligation with one person behind it is a daily obligation
  that has holidays.
- **You report things that make you look bad.** A supervisor whose record is
  clean is either extraordinary or not looking.
- **Nothing is normalised.** The measure is whether something that would have
  stopped work in January still stops work in August.
- **Your escalation path fires occasionally.** If nothing has ever escalated
  from you, either you are perfect or you are absorbing it, and absorbing it is
  how one person's overload becomes an organisation's blind spot.

## How it goes wrong

- **Pencil-whipping.** Filling in checks that were not made. It begins as
  catching up one form on a bad day, and it is the single most destructive thing
  anybody in this position can do, because it converts the organisation's whole
  evidence base into something nobody can trust — including the parts that were
  honest.
- **Production pressure absorbed silently.** The schedule is somebody else's
  problem until a supervisor quietly decides to carry it, and then it is
  nobody's problem and everybody's risk.
- **Being the only person who knows.** Where the hazard is, who is new, which
  piece of equipment is temperamental — held in one head, and gone with a
  transfer or a bad week.
- **Doing the crew's job instead of supervising it.** The most competent people
  fail this way, and it is hard to criticise in the moment. Supervision is not a
  thing you do between tasks; the moment it becomes that, nobody is watching.
- **Treating a near miss as a non-event.** It is the cheapest information this
  organisation will ever be offered, and it is offered to you and to nobody else.

## What you cannot hand to anybody else

Stopping work. Everything else in this position can be covered, deputised or
rescheduled. The decision that something is not safe to continue is a
supervisor's, is required to be available in the moment, and cannot wait for a
call to be returned. A supervisor who can only recommend is a supervisor who
will eventually document a hazard nobody stopped.

Also: telling a worker something they did was unsafe. Delegating that upward
turns a correction into a disciplinary process, and delegating it sideways
teaches the crew that you did not think it mattered.
