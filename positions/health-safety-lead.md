## What this job is for

To know, at any moment, what is actually happening to people at work — and to
be able to show it. Not to write the programme: to operate it. The documents are
how the organisation states what it does; this job is the reason any of it is
true. Almost everything in it is a form of *looking*, and almost every failure
of it is a form of not having looked.

## Your first week

Do these in order. Each one tells you whether the next is worth doing.

1. **Find out what is late.** Not what exists — what is late. A programme with
   every document written and half its records missing is in worse shape than
   one with three documents and every record filed, because the first looks
   finished.
2. **Find out who is a committee member or representative, and whether they
   know.** The worker side of this programme is the half that is most often
   nominally staffed. A name on a poster is not a person who has been trained,
   paid for the time, and given the information they are entitled to.
3. **Read the last twelve months of incidents, including the ones with no
   injury.** If the near misses and first aids are missing, that is your first
   finding and it is bigger than anything in the register.
4. **Ask a supervisor to walk you through one shift's paperwork.** Whatever they
   actually do is what the programme actually is. The gap between that and the
   written procedure is your workload for the quarter.
5. **Find out what escalates to you and how fast.** You will be interrupted by
   other people's lateness far more than by your own work, and on a clock you
   did not set. Know the shortest one before it fires.
6. **Only now** read the documents. You will read them differently, and you will
   notice which ones were written to be read by an auditor rather than by a
   worker.

## What good looks like

- **The same finding does not appear twice.** An item that recurs on
  consecutive inspections is not a finding being managed, it is a finding being
  re-documented. One repeat is an oversight; three is the programme telling you
  it does not close anything.
- **Near misses outnumber injuries, by a lot.** A rising near-miss count is
  almost always good news: it means people are telling you things. A falling
  one, with injuries flat, means they have stopped.
- **Somebody other than you can answer an auditor.** If every question routes
  to you, the programme is a person rather than a system, and it ends when you
  do.
- **Work stops sometimes.** A programme in which nothing is ever stopped is not
  a programme with no hazards; it is one where stopping is not really available.
- **The records were made when the work happened.** Anything filled in on Friday
  for the whole week is fiction, however accurate.

## How it goes wrong

- **Paper compliance.** Everything exists, everything is signed, and nothing is
  read. This is the most common failure and the hardest to see from inside,
  because every individual artifact is correct. The tell is that no document has
  changed in two years.
- **The programme becomes yours.** Duties belong to supervisors, to employers,
  to the committee — and drift to whoever is willing to carry them. A health and
  safety lead who is personally performing the inspections has quietly taken a
  duty off the person the law put it on, and the organisation now has no second
  line at all.
- **Under-reporting through the reporting route.** Routing reports through a
  supervisor under-reports exactly the events supervisors are involved in. It
  looks like an improvement for about a year.
- **Chasing the certificate instead of the risk.** Certification asks whether the
  system runs; it cannot ask whether it runs on the right things. Both matter and
  only one has a deadline, so only one gets done.
- **Treating the trailing numbers as the programme.** Injury rates measure last
  year. By the time they move, the thing that moved them has been happening for
  months.
- **Silent acceptance of a missing record.** A record that was never made is not
  neutral. It is the absence of the only evidence that the work happened, and it
  is indistinguishable, later, from the work not happening.

## What you cannot hand to anybody else

Two things, and they are the two that look most delegable.

**The judgement that a hazard is controlled.** Somebody else can inspect,
measure, and write it up. Deciding that what they found is acceptable is a
position's judgement, and if you sign it having not understood it, you have
converted somebody else's observation into your organisation's conclusion.

**Telling senior management something they do not want to hear, in writing, in
time.** This is the whole of the job at the moment it matters. Everything else
in this brief is machinery that exists so that when the moment comes you have
something to point at other than your own opinion.
