What this document must establish for THIS organisation: what it means to be fit
to perform safety-sensitive work, and what happens when somebody is not.

Named for the duty rather than for the substance, deliberately. Impairment has
several sources — alcohol, drugs including prescribed ones, fatigue, acute illness
— and a document written only about two of them leaves a supervisor with no basis
to act on the shift-worker who has been awake for nineteen hours.

It must define which roles are SAFETY-SENSITIVE and why, because the obligations
that follow are proportionate to that and to nothing else. A blanket programme
applied to every role is both harder to defend and easier to challenge.

It must state what a supervisor does when they have reasonable grounds to believe
somebody is unfit: what is said, who is called, how the person gets home, and that
the immediate concern is the person's safety rather than an investigation.

It must be explicit that dependence is a protected condition in most
jurisdictions, and that a request for help is treated differently from a refusal
to comply. A programme that cannot tell those apart will discipline the disclosure
it needed to receive.

Where testing is part of the programme, the document must say when it is
permitted, on what grounds, who may see the result and how long it is kept — and
it must record where that authority comes from, because in several jurisdictions
it does not exist. This is the artifact most likely to be constrained by law
rather than by the standard, and the register of legal requirements is where those
constraints are tracked.
