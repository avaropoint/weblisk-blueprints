What this document must establish for THIS organisation: what the public is
told, in language they can read, about what happens to information about them.

It must say what is collected, why, what it is used for, who it is shared with,
where it goes, how long it is kept, and how to reach the person accountable for
it. Those are the openness and identifying-purposes principles, and every one of
them has to be answerable from the personal information inventory — a notice
that describes practices the inventory does not contain is a statement somebody
wrote rather than a description of what happens.

It must be specific about disclosure to third parties, including service
providers outside Canada. There is no consent requirement for a transfer to a
processor under PIPEDA — a transfer for processing is a use, not a disclosure —
but the Commissioner's position is that the organisation must be transparent
about it, and being transparent means naming that the information may be stored
or processed outside Canada and is then subject to that country's law.

It must be written for the person reading it. A notice that meets every legal
requirement and cannot be understood has failed the consent requirement, because
consent is only valid if it is knowledgeable.

It must say how consent is obtained and how it can be withdrawn, and the
withdrawal route must work. An unsubscribe link that returns an error is a CASL
contravention and a PIPEDA one at the same moment.

Where the organisation makes decisions about people using automated processing,
it must say so. That is a requirement in Quebec under Law 25 rather than under
PIPEDA — and an organisation with customers in Quebec is bound by it — but it is
also the disclosure people most often complain about not having had.

It carries no obligation of its own: the notice is reviewed as part of the
annual privacy programme review, and giving it a second annual cycle would
produce two occurrences for one act.
